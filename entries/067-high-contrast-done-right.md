# 067 — High contrast done right

> Entry 067 — added 2026-11-14 (Drop 6: Structure, comfort, and trust).

*Contrast is the difference between text you can read and text you can only guess at.*

**What it is.** High contrast done right means every piece of text and every control stands far enough apart from its background in lightness that a person with reduced vision can still read it and find it, and it means the app survives the operating system's own high contrast mode instead of falling apart under it. On Windows, a contrast theme (still called High Contrast Mode in older versions) tells the browser or app to throw away most author colors and paint with a small user-chosen palette instead. On the web that surfaces as forced-colors mode: the browser forces text, background, border, and outline colors to system colors, forces box-shadow and text-shadow to none, and drops non-URL background images. An app that drew its buttons with shadows instead of borders, or that carried meaning in color alone, comes out of that process with invisible controls. A high contrast theme done right is a separate, deliberate palette where borders are real, text is dark on light or light on dark with room to spare, and nothing depends on a subtle shade to be understood. VS Code ships exactly this: a built-in High Contrast theme on all platforms, plus a terminal setting that lifts dim colors until they hit a chosen contrast ratio.

**Why it feels good.** Low contrast is not a style choice for the person reading it, it is a wall. WCAG's own rationale says the 4.5:1 text minimum exists to compensate for the contrast sensitivity loss of vision around 20/40, roughly what a typical 80-year-old has, and the 7:1 enhanced level covers vision around 20/80. People with moderately low vision do not use contrast-enhancing assistive technology for ordinary reading, so the app itself has to supply the contrast. When it does, the interface feels crisp and certain for everyone: buttons look like buttons, focus is unmistakable, and nothing has to be squinted at. And when the user turns on their OS contrast theme, an app that respects it feels like it was built for them, because in a real sense it was built to let their settings win.

**The numbers.** WCAG 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), Level AA, requires text and images of text to reach at least 4.5:1 against their background, with large text (18 pt, or 14 pt bold, about 24 px and 18.5 px) allowed 3:1. The ratios are hard thresholds: 4.499:1 fails 4.5:1, no rounding up. Logos and logotypes are exempt, decorative and inactive text is exempt, and brand colors are not exempt beyond the logo itself. The rule also covers placeholder text and text shown on hover or focus. WCAG 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced), Level AAA, raises the bar to 7:1 for normal text and 4.5:1 for large text. WCAG 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast, Level AA, requires 3:1 for the visual parts of interface components and meaningful graphics against adjacent colors, including the focus indicator, the check in a checkbox, and the thumb of a slider. A control needs a visible boundary only when there is no other way to tell it is there, but the focus indicator always needs its 3:1. In forced-colors mode, MDN documents that box-shadow and text-shadow are forced to none and non-URL background images are forced to none, which is why MDN's own example replaces a shadow-drawn button edge with a 2 px solid border in the system ButtonText color. Firefox adds that Windows high contrast lets users override seven colors and Firefox's own high contrast mode four, and that macOS Increase Contrast does not trigger forced-colors at all because it does not limit the palette. Firefox's prefers-contrast reports more when the user's foreground/background ratio is above 7:1, less when it is below 4.5, and custom in between. VS Code's terminal minimum contrast setting takes any number from 1 to 21 and adjusts text luminance until the ratio is met or pure white or black is reached.

**Where it fits his apps.** ACR: the resonance trace and its flag markers need real contrast against the chart background, and the focus ring on chart controls must be a visible border, not a color shift alone. MIMIK: the capture button and its recording state must read at a glance, and the status text must hold 4.5:1 against its panel. GITDATA: dataset status and sync state must not be carried by a pale green versus pale red alone; pair the color with an icon or a word. Qwkhnd: task states and priorities need borders and labels, not tint-only chips. In Qt, a high contrast theme is a palette swap, which is cheap to ship, but two Qt facts matter: a focus ring must be a border on :focus, because outline renders 0 pixels in this build, and any control whose only boundary is a shadow will look borderless in a high contrast palette, so draw real borders from the start.

**Watch out.** Do not draw a control's edge with a shadow: forced-colors mode deletes shadows, and the control vanishes into its background. Do not put words in images: images of text pixelate when scaled, cannot be recolored by the user's theme, and still have to meet the same 4.5:1. Do not treat a separate high contrast theme as a license to ship a low contrast default: the default theme still has to meet 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for controls, and most users who need contrast never find the theme setting. Do not override the user's forced palette with forced-color-adjust: none except for content that is genuinely unreadable any other way; MDN warns that the high contrast palette and text backplates are often essential for the user to read the page at all. And do not assume a dark theme is a high contrast theme: dark gray text on a black background can fail 4.5:1 just as easily as light gray on white.
