# 013 — Diegetic UI

> Entry 013 — added 2026-11-13 (Drop 2: Games, editors, and gadgets).

*the interface lives inside the work*

**What it is.** The interface is part of the thing itself, not a layer on
top of it. No HUD, no menus floating over the work, no waypoints telling
you where to look. The health bar is a glowing spine on the character's
back (Dead Space). The settings are the machine you are operating. The
2026 horror game MOLE takes it all the way: fully diegetic, no HUD, no
menus, no waypoints, no tutorial prompts. The game opens by making you
insert a cassette, and that action is both the story and the controls.

**Why it feels good.** Nothing sits between you and the work. There is no
chrome to learn, no panel to open, no mode to switch into. You touch the
thing itself. MOLE's demo earned 97 percent positive across 180 Steam
ratings in January 2026, with 100 percent positive from the 55 most recent
reviews, and the diegetic design is what reviewers kept naming.

**The numbers.**
- MOLE demo, January 29, 2026: 97 percent positive, 180 Steam ratings;
  100 percent positive from the 55 most recent.
- The idea is not new: the diegetic interface framework was codified in a
  2009 thesis at Chalmers University. What is new is games shipping it
  all the way, with no fallback HUD at all.

**Where it fits his apps.** Yahtzee (the scorecard IS the interface: the
dice cup, the score sheet, nothing else), Reblend (the 3D scene is the UI:
drag the object, not a slider), LYNX SHARE (the mixer strip is the thing
itself), Pixel Forge (canvas first, tools on the canvas, panels only when
asked for).

**Watch out.** Do not hide urgent information inside the fiction. Health,
errors, and anything the user must not miss need to be in plain sight,
even if that means a small honest overlay. Diegetic for the work itself,
plain for the warnings.
