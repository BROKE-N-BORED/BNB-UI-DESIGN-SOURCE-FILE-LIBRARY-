# 061 — Feedback loops

> Entry 061 — added 2026-11-14 (Drop 6: Structure, comfort, and trust).

*Every action should answer back, so the user always knows what happened and what to do next.*

**What it is.** A feedback loop is the round trip between a user and a system: the user acts, the system shows what happened, the user reads that result and acts again. Don Norman's human action cycle, also called the Seven Stages of Action, is the frame for it. The cycle runs in three phases: forming a goal, executing (turning the goal into tasks, sequencing them, carrying them out), then evaluating (perceiving what happened, interpreting it against expectations, comparing it with what was intended). Two gulfs can break the loop. The gulf of execution is the gap between what the user intends and the actions the system makes available. The gulf of evaluation is the gap between what the system did and the user's ability to tell what happened. The terms were coined in 1986 by Ed Hutchins, Jim Hollan, and Don Norman, writing about direct manipulation interfaces. Norman's four principles for closing the gulfs are visibility, a good conceptual model, good mappings, and feedback, where feedback means the user receives full and continuous feedback about the results of their actions. This entry is the theory behind Joshua's own rule U16, "always show what it's doing", and the frame that unifies the feedback pieces the library already covers separately: micro-interactions (001), skeleton screens (011), toasts (030), inline validation (044), and response-time limits (046).

**Why it matters.** A loop with a missing step is a broken interface, and the break is diagnosable. For every action in an app, ask three questions: can the user perceive the result, can they make sense of it, and can they compare it with what they intended? If any answer is no, that is where the UI is broken. Kathryn Whitenton's NN/g account of her own Bluetooth headset is the textbook case: she spent over an hour trying to connect it, re-reading the manual and testing the headset on another computer, because the Windows 10 Bluetooth switch showed the label Off next to a slider, and she read the label as the action the switch would take rather than the state it was in. The status was visible. It was not interpretable. Visibility without interpretation is the most common form of the failure, and it is invisible to the team that built it, because they already know what the signals mean. A well-designed product lets the user complete the cycle once per action. Needing several cycles for one action means frustration at best.

**The key facts.** The seven stages are goal formation, translation into tasks, sequencing, execution, perceiving results, interpreting outcomes, and comparing with intent. Evaluation itself has two subtasks, perceiving the indicator and interpreting what it means, and success at one does not guarantee success at the other. The gulfs are interdependent: successful execution usually depends on correct evaluation, so a plan built on a faulty reading of the system state is doomed before it starts. Feedback has a measured effect on patience. In a 2004 study at the University of Nebraska-Lincoln, reported by Nielsen Norman Group, people who saw a moving progress bar while a page loaded were willing to wait on average 3 times longer than people who saw no indicator, and reported higher satisfaction. The shipping example is LinkedIn's profile strength meter: the long silent form, which gave no sign of how much was left, became a meter that fills with each field added and names the stages from Beginner through Intermediate, Advanced, and Expert to All-Star, so every small action answers back with visible progress. The meter also starts part-filled, never at zero. The psychology behind it is the endowed progress effect, documented by Nunes and Drèze in 2006: in a field experiment at a car wash, 300 customers got loyalty cards, half needing eight stamps and half needing ten with two already stamped, so both groups needed eight more washes. Completion was 34 percent for the pre-stamped cards against 19 percent for the blank ones, a statistically significant gap, and the pre-stamped customers finished faster. The mechanism is the goal-gradient effect, proposed by Clark Hull in 1932: effort rises as the goal gets closer. The timings for feedback live in entry 046; the loop is the reason those timings matter.

**Where it fits his apps.** ACR: analysis over a file or folder must report each file as it is processed, with how many remain, so the analyst can watch the loop close on every step instead of staring at a frozen window while the trace stays empty. MIMIK: the capture button must say capturing now in plain text, with a running timer, and a clip must answer with a saved confirmation; a silent capture is a gulf of evaluation with a record button on it. GITDATA: indexing and syncing report real counts as they go, and a save shows as saved in the row itself, so the user can compare what they changed against what the store now holds. Qwkhnd: every triage action confirms in place, "Moved to Done" with Undo beside it, and a rebuild shows its real progress rather than a blank list. In Qt, feedback is text and state, not motion: this PySide6 build has no cheap transform-animation path, so the loop closes with a status line, a state label, and a progress bar with real numbers. A focus ring that never appears is a broken loop too, so focus rings are a border on :focus, since outline renders 0 pixels in this build.

**What it gets wrong.** Feedback that cannot be interpreted is not feedback. The Bluetooth switch proves it: the label was there, the state was visible, and the user still spent an hour lost, because the signal matched the wrong mental model. Feedback that lies is worse than none: a progress bar that races to 99 percent and hangs there destroys the trust it was built to create, and Nielsen Norman Group warns the benefit is negated when speed changes are noticed. Feedback for very fast actions is noise: a spinner on a sub-second wait flashes like a glitch, so short waits need only the result itself. Static feedback is barely feedback at all: a frozen "Loading..." gives no way to tell a slow system from a stuck one, and one user in an NN/g testing session waited 15 minutes for an app that had stopped. And the loop has an end state to design for: motivation drops immediately after a goal is reached, even with a second reward in sight, so the moment after completion needs its own next step, not a dead end.
