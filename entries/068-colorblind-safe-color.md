# 068 — Colorblind-safe color

> Entry 068 — added 2026-11-14 (Drop 6: Structure, comfort, and trust).

*Color can carry the message, but it must never be the only thing carrying it.*

**What it is.** Colorblind-safe color is two habits at once. The first is choosing colors that stay distinguishable to people with color vision deficiency, which mostly means red-green deficiency: avoid red versus green as the only difference, differ on lightness as well as hue, and use a tested palette like Okabe-Ito when categories need distinct colors. The second, and the one that matters more, is redundant coding: every meaning color carries must also be carried by shape, icon, pattern, label, or position, so the screen still makes sense in grayscale. Okabe and Ito, both strong protanopes themselves, put it as three principles plus one: choose schemes identifiable by all types of color vision, combine color with shapes, positions, line types, and patterns, state color names where communication depends on them, and aim for designs that are visually friendly and beautiful. Games that do this well give each gem type a distinct shape, mark each faction with an outline or icon as well as a color, and treat a colorblind mode as a supplement to a design that already works, not as the fix for one that does not.

**Why it feels good.** About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of color vision deficiency, so in any real audience the colorblind reader is already there. When color is the only carrier, that reader does not get a degraded version of the information, they get none of it: the red enemy health bar and the green friendly one become the same murky bar, and a chart whose only key is color becomes a guessing game. Redundant coding costs almost nothing and pays everyone back: a label, an icon, or a dash pattern makes the interface clearer for the fully sighted reader too, because a second channel is simply more legible than one. And a tested palette like Okabe-Ito removes the guesswork from picking category colors, so the designer stops worrying whether two series will collide and gets on with the work.

**The numbers.** Okabe and Ito's own figures: 1 in 12 Caucasian males (8 percent), 1 in 20 Asian males (5 percent), and 1 in 25 African males (4 percent) are red-green colorblind, and tritanopia (blue-yellow) occurs in only 0.001 percent. In a room of 250 people there should be more than 10 colorblind people, and in a product used by 10,000 people, about 400 of them would be colorblind. WizusLabs adds the women's figure: about 1 in 200 women (0.5 percent). The Okabe-Ito palette, published on Okabe and Ito's Color Universal Design page in 2002 and revised in 2008, is eight colors: orange #E69F00, sky blue #56B4E9, bluish green #009E73, yellow #F0E442, blue #0072B2, vermillion #D55E00, reddish purple #CC79A7, and black #000000, with an optional grey #999999 as a ninth. (The original page shows the palette as an image; the hex values are from the Vizcept reference, which reproduces the published RGB values.) It is a categorical palette with a hard cap of eight groups. Not all eight work as text: on a white background only blue (5.19:1) and black clear WCAG's 4.5:1 for text and thin lines, while yellow manages only 1.32:1, so yellow belongs on filled areas and thick bars, never on labels. Two pairs are nearly identical in lightness, orange and sky blue (L* 70.6 vs 69.8) and bluish green and vermillion (57.7 vs 54.2), so those pairs need different marker shapes or line styles. WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Color, Level A, states the rule in one sentence: color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response, or distinguishing a visual element. A lightness difference counts as a second channel only when the contrast ratio between the colors is at least 3:1, and even then, a green-valid versus red-invalid outline still needs an additional indicator, because the meaning depends on naming the color.

**Where it fits his apps.** ACR: the resonance trace uses Okabe-Ito colors for its series, each with its own line style and a label on the chart itself rather than a distant color key, and flag markers carry an icon as well as a color. GITDATA: dataset status shows an icon plus a word (Synced, Stale, Failed), never a colored dot alone, and coverage charts use patterns as well as hues. MIMIK: the recording state is a red dot plus the word REC plus a timer, so the state survives even if the red reads as dark gray. Qwkhnd: priority is a shape and a label (a filled triangle for urgent, a plain row for routine), not a red versus green chip. Yahtzee: dice already pass, because pips are shape, but any color-coded scoring highlight needs the same treatment. In Qt this is nearly free: the palette is eight hex values in the token file, and the redundant channel is usually an icon or a label the layout already has room for.

**Watch out.** Do not use the Okabe-Ito palette as a gradient: its colors are maximally different, not stepwise, so picking three or four of them as a low to high scale misleads the reader; use a sequential palette for ordered data. Do not put yellow or sky blue on thin lines or small text: at 1.32:1 and 2.31:1 on white they fail text contrast badly. Do not ship a colorblind mode and call the job done: a game or app that is unplayable with the toggle off has only moved the problem behind a menu the user may never find. Do not use red characters on a black background: protanopes see dark red as almost black, and Okabe and Ito warn against it explicitly. And do not trust a colorblind simulator as the whole test: the simulators mimic the most strongly affected dichromats, and Okabe and Ito advise asking colorblind people to examine the design, since many distinguish more than the simulation shows.
