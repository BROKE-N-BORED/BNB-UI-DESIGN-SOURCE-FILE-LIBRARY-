# 064 — Density controls

> Entry 064 — added 2026-11-14 (Drop 6: Structure, comfort, and trust).

*Let the user choose how much fits on screen: comfortable for reading, compact for scanning.*

**What it is.** A density control is a user setting, usually two or three named levels, that changes how much space each row or item takes in a list, table, or inbox. Gmail offers three in Quick settings under Density: Default, Comfortable, and Compact, and Default also shows a preview of attachments from the inbox. VS Code offers a layout density of Default or Compact, where Compact removes the spacing between panels and reduces the spacing within them; the choice applies immediately and persists when the app restarts. MUI's data grid offers compact, standard, or comfortable row density. What changes is spacing, row heights and padding, not the text: Material Design 3 says text size should not change as the container scales. The point is not to cram more in for its own sake. It is to let the person match the screen to the job: scanning a hundred rows wants compact, reading a few rows carefully wants comfortable, and the same person wants both at different times.

**Why it feels good.** Density is a preference, and preferences differ by person, by task, and by screen. Someone triaging a long list wants as many rows visible as possible, because every scroll is a context switch. Someone reading carefully wants air around each row, because crowded rows blur together. A single fixed density serves one of those people and annoys the other. Giving the choice costs almost nothing: it is a spacing change, not a redesign, and it applies instantly, so the user can try it and revert it in seconds. It also respects a real finding: Nielsen Norman Group's testing found that low information density does not make for good usability, and users were quick to reject designs too sparse to be useful, especially for portal and news sites where scanning is the job. Sparse is not automatically better. Dense is not automatically worse. The right density is the one the person chose for the job in front of them, and the app should remember it.

**The numbers.** Material Design 3 numbers its component density scale from 0, the default, down to -1, -2, and -3 as space decreases, and each step decreases the top and bottom padding or overall height by 4dp. Its floor is firm: the default target size should be at least 48 by 48 CSS pixels, component scaling must not be applied by default if it would push a target below 48 by 48, layouts must not be scaled below 48 by 48dp by default, and the density setting controls themselves must use default target sizes so the setting can always be reverted. IBM's Carbon system publishes the most complete row-height scale: extra small 24px, small 32px, medium 40px, large 48px, and extra large 64px, with the column header row always matching the body row height, the extra large size recommended only when cells hold two lines of content, the 48px toolbar paired with large and extra large rows, the small toolbar with small and extra small rows, and pagination height always matching the row height. MUI's data grid defaults to 52px rows, which it says matches the normal height in the Material Design guidelines, and offers compact, standard, and comfortable density from there. Gmail publishes no pixel heights for its three densities, so this entry gives none: the mechanism is three named levels in Quick settings, with Default adding attachment previews. VS Code's layout density is also mechanism-only in its docs: Default uses standard spacing, Compact removes spacing between panels and reduces spacing within them, and it currently sits behind the experimental modern UI setting. Nielsen Norman Group's density finding rests on a small study, 12 users, 6 Chinese and 6 expatriate, each completing two tasks on 8 websites, so treat it as direction, not as a precise measure.

**Where it fits his apps.** GITDATA: dataset and search-result lists are the clearest fit, with a comfortable default around 48px rows and a compact opt-in around 32px for scanning a long index, following Carbon's scale. Qwkhnd: the task list gets the same two levels, compact for fast triage, comfortable as the default, since triage actions are tapped in rapid succession and the row is the target. ACR: the trace log is a long list of steps, so compact shows more of a run at once, while the resonance chart and the entry detail stay comfortable, because those are for reading, not scanning. MIMIK: the capture library list, compact for finding a clip among many, comfortable for reading clip notes. Code Forge: the findings list, compact when a review has dozens of findings to scan. Fyrn: the model list, where compact fits more models on one screen. Density should never change what a row does, only how much room it takes, and the choice should persist per app, the way VS Code's persists across restarts. Qt fit: density is a spacing token set, not motion, so it is cheap on his PC: one comfortable and one compact set of paddings and row heights, applied through the stylesheet or the item delegate, with no animation at all. Keep the text size identical between densities, keep every clickable target at its full hit area even when the row shrinks, and keep the focus ring as a border on :focus, which still reads at compact row heights. A dialog that offers a density switch must scroll if the comfortable setting makes its content taller than the dialog.

**Watch out.** Compact is an opt-in, never the default. Material Design 3 says people should be able to opt in to dense layouts, and warns that dense components can be less accessible because interactive elements get smaller. The line between compact and cramped is the target: a row can shrink, but the thing inside it that gets clicked must keep a usable hit area, 48 by 48dp in Material's terms, 24 by 24 CSS pixels as the WCAG 2.2 floor. Do not densify the places where a mistake costs something: Material Design 3 says not to increase density in UIs that involve focused tasks, such as selecting from a menu, where it shrinks the selectable space, and not in components that alert a person to changes, such as snackbars or dialogs. Do not let density change on its own: it should not automatically shift across breakpoints or orientation unless the person changes it. Keep the scale discrete, a few named levels with fixed heights, not a freeform slider, and keep the header row the same height as the body rows, as Carbon requires. If a row needs two lines of text, it needs the tall size, not the compact one. And remember what the NN/g study also found: the same users who liked density spent more time finding things and showed more hesitation on the most complicated sites, so density serves scanning, it does not excuse clutter.
