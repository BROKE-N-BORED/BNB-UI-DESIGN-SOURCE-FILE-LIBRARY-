# 051 — Overview first, details on demand

> Entry 051 — added 2026-11-13 (Drop 5: Choices, motion, and craft).

*Show the whole first, then let people narrow in and ask for detail only when they need it.*

**What it is.** The visual information-seeking mantra, from Ben Shneiderman's 1996 paper "The Eyes Have It: A Task by Data Type Taxonomy for Information Visualizations" (Proceedings of the 1996 IEEE Symposium on Visual Languages; University of Maryland tech report CS-TR-3665, July 1996): "Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand." Shneiderman printed the phrase ten times in the paper, one line for each project in which he had found himself rediscovering the principle. The mantra is one piece of a larger taxonomy. The paper classifies data into seven types (1-, 2-, and 3-dimensional, temporal, multidimensional, tree, and network) and names seven tasks a user performs on any of them: overview, zoom, filter, details-on-demand, relate, history, and extract. The mantra covers the first four. Each level answers one question and hands off to the next: the overview asks "is anything off?", zoom and filter ask "where?", details ask "which records, and why?"

**Why it matters.** It is the order that makes a large collection explorable without overwhelming the person exploring it. The overview gives orientation and reveals patterns, clusters, gaps, and outliers that no list of rows ever shows. Zoom and filter narrow to the part that matters, without losing the sense of the whole. Details arrive only on demand, so the main view stays readable and the app only fetches row-level data for the one item someone clicked, instead of loading everything on open. That last point is a performance strategy as much as a design one, and it suits a no-GPU machine well: aggregates and counts are cheap to draw, full detail is rendered only when asked for. The three tasks the mantra leaves out are what separate a usable analytical tool from a pretty chart: relate (see relationships among items), history (keep a record of actions for undo, replay, and step-by-step refinement), and extract (pull out a subset of the data and the query settings that produced it).

**The key facts.** This entry is mechanism-only: the mantra is a design guideline drawn from building and reviewing visualization systems, not the result of a controlled experiment, and a 2005 review by Brock Craft and Paul Cairns found that papers citing the mantra rarely tested it empirically. Treat it as well-worn practitioner wisdom, not measured fact. The seven tasks, as the paper defines them: overview (gain an overview of the entire collection), zoom (zoom in on items of interest), filter (filter out uninteresting items), details-on-demand (select an item or group and get details when needed), relate (view relationships among items), history (keep a history of actions to support undo, replay, and progressive refinement), and extract (allow extraction of sub-collections and of the query parameters). Three later variants reorder the mantra for cases it handles badly. Keim and colleagues (2006) proposed an "analyse first" mantra for data too large to inspect directly: "Analyse first, show the important, zoom, filter and analyse further, details on demand." Van Ham and Perer (2009) proposed "search, show context, expand on demand" for huge networks where the user starts from a known item. Segel and Heer (2010) described the "martini glass" structure for narrative visualization: an author-driven sequence first, then an open, reader-driven exploration stage.

**Where it fits his apps.** ACR: the trace overview first (the whole run as one timeline), scrub to a region of interest, click a span for its details. GITDATA: a repo overview (recent activity, branch health), filter by author or time, click a commit for the diff. Code Forge: a review run summary (how many issues, by severity), filter by file, click an issue for the fix. Data Pack Builder: a pack overview (counts by type), filter to one category, click an item to edit. Yahtzee: the scorecard as the overview, tap a category for its scoring detail. Fyrn: the model library as an overview (installed, available, sizes), filter by size or task, click a model for its full details. In every case the overview is aggregates and counts, cheap to compute and cheap to draw, and the expensive rendering waits behind a click.

**What it gets wrong.** The mantra assumes the full dataset can be summarized on one screen in a way that is both readable and truthful, and that assumption breaks often. A scatter plot of ten million points overplots into a blob, and a network with millions of nodes renders as a hairball: the "overview" hides more than it shows, and Keim's analyse-first order fits better. If the user arrives with a known item in hand, a search box beats the prettiest overview, which is the van Ham and Perer case. Aggregation can hide the signal: a flat company total can mask one region up sharply and another down just as sharply, so the most important pattern never reaches the screen. Treating "overview" as "show everything" is the commonest failure: a landing page with 30 charts is not an overview, it is a wall. Details behind hover only are unreachable on touchscreens and from the keyboard, so always provide a click or tap path to the same detail. And skipping history and extract makes the drill-down work once: the user finds something, cannot back out cleanly, and cannot hand the exact view to anyone else. Finally, the mantra is for exploration, not communication. When the goal is to tell someone what the data says, the martini glass order (narrative first, exploration second) fits better than an open-ended overview.
